In most U.S. jurisdictions, yes: a law firm can generally use outside or remote nonlawyer staff for intake and client communication, as long as the attorneys supervise the work and protect client confidentiality. The ABA Model Rules address this mainly in Rule 5.3 (responsibilities regarding nonlawyer assistance) and Rule 1.6 (confidentiality of information), and most states have adopted similar rules, sometimes with their own ethics opinions on outsourcing. Whether it is safe depends on how you set it up: confidentiality terms, controlled access to your systems, written scripts, clear limits on legal advice and real supervision. This article is general information, not legal advice; check your state bar's rules and guidance before you decide.

## What ABA Model Rule 5.3 says, in plain terms

Rule 5.3 covers nonlawyers who help a lawyer, whether they are employees or people working for an outside provider. In general terms, it asks three things of the firm:

- **Firm-level measures.** Partners and managers must make reasonable efforts to have measures in place that give reasonable assurance that nonlawyers' conduct is compatible with the lawyers' professional obligations.
- **Direct supervision.** A lawyer who directly supervises a nonlawyer must make reasonable efforts to make sure that person's conduct is compatible with those obligations.
- **Responsibility.** A lawyer can be responsible for a nonlawyer's conduct that would break the rules if the lawyer ordered or approved it, or knew about it in time and failed to take reasonable remedial action.

The official comments to Rule 5.3 specifically discuss nonlawyers outside the firm. They say the reasonable efforts required depend on things like the provider's education, experience and reputation, the kind of work, the confidentiality terms in the arrangement, and the legal and ethical environment where the work is done. In short, using outside help is anticipated by the rule; the duty to supervise it is not optional.

## What Rule 1.6 says about client information

Rule 1.6 says a lawyer generally may not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or a listed exception applies. Rule 1.6(c) adds that a lawyer must make reasonable efforts to prevent the inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to, that information.

Intake staff hear exactly this kind of information: injuries, medical treatment, employment problems, immigration-related worries, finances. Sharing it with staff who help carry out the representation is normally part of running a practice, but the firm has to protect it with reasonable safeguards. The ABA has also discussed outsourcing directly in Formal Opinion 08-451 (2008), and some guidance says that in certain situations the firm should tell clients about outside help or get their consent. Your state's position is what counts.

## Intake vs. legal advice: where the line sits

The biggest practical risk with any intake person, in-house or remote, is drifting into legal advice. A caller asks "Do I have a case?" or "Should I talk to the insurance adjuster?" and a helpful staff member answers. That answer can be the unauthorized practice of law and can create problems for the firm.

A good intake setup makes the line obvious:

- Staff collect facts, follow the firm's screening script and book consultations.
- Staff use approved wording for common legal questions, for example: "That's a question for the attorney. Let me get you a consultation so they can answer it."
- The attorney evaluates the matter, decides whether the firm takes it and explains fees and next steps.

## Practical safeguards that make outsourcing safe

Whether you hire one remote assistant or a team, these are the safeguards most firms put in place:

1. **Confidentiality terms in writing.** A nondisclosure or confidentiality agreement covering client information, during and after the engagement. Ask any provider, including YVA, for its confidentiality terms in writing.
2. **Individual accounts you control.** Each person gets their own login to your CRM, case management system and email, with multi-factor authentication. No shared passwords.
3. **Least access.** Intake staff see what intake needs. They do not need access to every case file or the firm's bank account.
4. **Work stays in your systems.** Notes, documents and messages live in your firm's tools, such as Clio, MyCase, HubSpot, Salesforce, Slack or Google Workspace, not in personal email, personal phones or downloads.
5. **Written scripts and rules.** An approved intake script, eligibility rules, escalation steps for urgent calls and approved answers to common legal questions.
6. **Conflict checks done by the firm.** Staff collect the names of everyone involved; the firm runs the conflict check and makes the decision before any substantive conversation goes further.
7. **A named supervising attorney.** One person reviews intake records, answers escalations and gives feedback, especially in the first weeks.
8. **Regular review.** A weekly look at a sample of calls or intake records, and a quick fix to the script when something goes wrong.
9. **Clean offboarding.** When someone leaves, accounts are turned off the same day.

If you record calls, check your state's call-recording consent rules first; some states require consent from everyone on the call.

## Is a remote intake team less safe than an in-house one?

Not inherently. A front-desk employee with a shared password and a sticky note of client numbers is a bigger risk than a remote specialist with an individual account, multi-factor authentication and access limited to intake. What matters is the controls, the supervision and the people. That is also why the comments to Rule 5.3 point to the provider's reputation, training and confidentiality arrangements.

When you compare providers, ask how staff are vetted and trained, how access is handled, what confidentiality terms they put in writing and how replacements work. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is it legal for a law firm to outsource intake?

Generally yes, under the ABA Model Rules, as long as the attorney supervises the nonlawyer staff (Rule 5.3) and protects client information (Rule 1.6). State rules and ethics opinions vary, so check your state bar's guidance. This is not legal advice.

### Can outsourced intake staff give legal advice?

No. Intake staff are nonlawyers. They collect facts, follow the firm's script and book consultations, and every legal question goes to an attorney.

### Do I need my clients' consent to use remote intake staff?

It depends on your state and the situation. Some guidance, including ABA Formal Opinion 08-451, says client disclosure or consent may be needed in certain outsourcing arrangements. Check your state bar's ethics opinions.

### Who runs conflict checks when intake is outsourced?

The firm. Intake staff collect the names of the people involved, and the firm runs the conflict check and decides whether it can take the matter.
